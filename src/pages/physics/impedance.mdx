Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy is usually simulated the way it is measured: apply a
small sinusoidal current, time-step the full nonlinear model until the transient dies, fit the
steady oscillation, move to the next frequency. At 10 mHz one point costs hundreds of seconds
of simulated time, and a decent spectrum needs fifty points. Voltaire does none of that. The
impedance of the discretised model is a property of its Jacobian, and the Jacobian is already
in hand.

## Impedance without time stepping

### Linearise about steady state

After spatial discretisation, every Voltaire model has the form

<Equation id="eq-dae" latex="M\,\frac{d\mathbf{u}}{dt} = F(\mathbf{u})" caption="The semi-discrete model: a differential-algebraic system. Rows of M are zero where the equation is algebraic, such as the potential equation." />

where $\mathbf{u}$ stacks every unknown — concentrations, potentials, surface coverages — and
$M$ is the (possibly singular) mass matrix. A singular $M$ is not a problem here; the
formalism below handles differential-algebraic systems as written.

First solve the steady state $F(\mathbf{u}^*) = 0$ with damped Newton. Then perturb it with a
small harmonic forcing $\hat{b}\,e^{i\omega t}$ — an applied current or voltage — and keep
only terms linear in the perturbation. The ansatz
$\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{u}^* + \hat{u}\,e^{i\omega t}$ turns the time derivative into a factor
of $i\omega$ and $F$ into its Jacobian, leaving one complex linear system per frequency:

<Equation id="eq-eis" latex="\left(i\omega M - J\right)\hat{u} = \hat{b}, \qquad J = \left.\frac{\partial F}{\partial \mathbf{u}}\right|_{\mathbf{u}^*}" caption="The frequency-domain EIS system. J is the same autodiff Jacobian that damped Newton uses to find the steady state." />

The impedance follows by reading the voltage and current phasors out of $\hat{u}$ and
$\hat{b}$:

<Equation id="eq-z" latex="Z(\omega) = \frac{\hat{V}(\omega)}{\hat{I}(\omega)}" caption="Impedance as the ratio of voltage to current phasors at the perturbation frequency." />

### One solve per frequency

Each frequency costs one sparse complex factorisation of $i\omega M - J$ — a matrix with
exactly the sparsity pattern of a single Newton step. Fifty log-spaced frequencies from
millihertz to hundreds of kilohertz are fifty such solves — designed to take seconds of wall
time on the discretisations Voltaire targets once the Python solver ships, where a transient
sweep spends hours waiting out low-frequency transients. There is no transient to converge,
no Fourier fit, and no sampling error: the linear response is exact to first order in the
perturbation amplitude.

The deeper point is consistency. Because $J$ is the same matrix Newton used, the spectrum you
compute is the small-signal response of exactly the nonlinear model you time-step — not of a
hand-fitted equivalent circuit that may or may not correspond to the physics. This is one arm
of the architecture described on the [numerics page](/Electrochemical-Cell-Simulator/numerics/):
one autodiff Jacobian consumed three ways — damped Newton for steady states, $(i\omega M - J)$
for impedance, and the adjoint system $J^{\mathsf{T}}\lambda = -\partial Q/\partial \mathbf{u}$
for design sensitivities. Implement the residual once, differentiate it once, and all three
consumers stay in agreement by construction.

## Distribution of relaxation times

A spectrum is a curve; the question is what processes are in it. The distribution of
relaxation times (DRT) represents the impedance as a superposition of first-order relaxations:

<Equation id="eq-drt" latex="Z(\omega) = R_{\infty} + \int_0^{\infty} \frac{\gamma(\tau)}{1 + i\omega\tau}\, d\tau" caption="DRT representation: the spectrum as a weighted continuum of RC-like relaxations. Peaks in γ(τ) mark distinct physical processes." />

Each timescale $\tau$ contributes an RC-like arc, and the weight function $\gamma(\tau)$ says
how much resistance relaxes at that timescale. Processes that overlap into a single depressed
arc on the Nyquist plot separate cleanly in $\gamma(\tau)$ because they live at different
timescales: charge transfer at microseconds to milliseconds, diffusion through surface films
at milliseconds to seconds, solid-state diffusion at seconds and beyond.

### The inversion is ill-posed

Recovering $\gamma(\tau)$ from measured $Z(\omega)$ is a Fredholm integral equation of the
first kind — the classic ill-posed problem, where noise at the level of the last digit can
produce arbitrarily large swings in the solution. Voltaire discretises $\tau$ on a log grid,
stacks the real and imaginary parts of the kernel into a matrix $A$, and solves a
Tikhonov-regularised non-negative least-squares problem:

<Equation id="eq-nnls" latex="\hat{\gamma} = \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\gamma \ge 0}\; \lVert A\gamma - z \rVert_2^2 + \lambda^2 \lVert L\gamma \rVert_2^2" caption="Tikhonov-NNLS inversion: fit the spectrum, penalise roughness, forbid negative resistance." />

The non-negativity constraint encodes that relaxing resistances are positive; the smoothing
operator $L$ (a discrete derivative on the log-τ grid) penalises oscillatory solutions.

  { s: '\\gamma(\\tau)', meaning: 'distribution of relaxation times', units: 'Ω/s' },
  { s: '\\tau', meaning: 'relaxation timescale', units: 's', typical: '1e-6 – 1e3' },
  { s: '\\lambda', meaning: 'Tikhonov regularisation strength', units: '—', typical: '1e-4 – 1e-1' },

### Choosing the regularisation strength

There is no neutral choice of $\lambda$, and it is dishonest to pretend otherwise. Too small,
and the inversion amplifies measurement noise into spurious sharp spikes that look like — but
are not — physical processes. Too large, and genuinely distinct peaks merge into one broad
hump, so you under-count the processes in the cell. Non-negativity suppresses the ringing
that plagues unconstrained Tikhonov inversion, but it cannot rescue a bad $\lambda$. The
[browser demo](/Electrochemical-Cell-Simulator/demos/#demo-impedance) puts $\lambda$ on a
slider precisely so you can watch both failure modes happen: drag it down until spikes
appear, drag it up until peaks fuse, and settle where the recovered peaks are stable over a
range of $\lambda$. That stability check, not any single magic value, is the defensible
practice.

## Analytic checks

Diffusion impedances have exact closed forms, which makes them sharp tests for both the
frequency-domain solver and the DRT pipeline.

### Semi-infinite Warburg

Diffusion into an unbounded medium gives

<Equation id="eq-warburg" latex="Z_W(\omega) = \frac{\sigma_W\,(1 - i)}{\sqrt{\omega}}" caption="Semi-infinite Warburg impedance: equal real and negative-imaginary parts at every frequency." />

Real and imaginary magnitudes are equal at every frequency, so the Nyquist trace is a straight
line at exactly 45° — a geometric fingerprint that any correct solver must reproduce and any
axis-scaling bug immediately destroys.

### Finite-length Warburg

When diffusion happens across a layer of finite thickness $L$, the impedance is

<Equation id="eq-fl-warburg" latex="Z_W(\omega) = R_d\,\frac{\tanh\sqrt{i\omega L^2 / D}}{\sqrt{i\omega L^2 / D}}" caption="Finite-length Warburg: 45° behaviour at high frequency, rolling over to the resistive limit R_d as ω → 0." />

At high frequency the diffusion front never feels the far boundary and the expression reduces
to the 45° semi-infinite line; below the characteristic frequency $\omega \sim D/L^2$ the
$\tanh$ saturates and the impedance bends over toward the purely resistive limit $R_d$. The
crossover frequency pins down $D/L^2$ directly from the spectrum.

## Reading a Nyquist plot

Throughout the site, Nyquist plots follow the electrochemists' convention: $-\mathrm{Im}\,Z$
on the vertical axis against $\mathrm{Re}\,Z$ on the horizontal, with equal scaling on both
axes. Capacitive processes have negative imaginary impedance, so plotting the negation puts
the familiar arcs above the axis. Equal axis scaling is not cosmetic: a semicircle is only a
semicircle, and a Warburg line is only at 45°, when one ohm spans the same distance in both
directions. Unequal scaling silently distorts exactly the geometric features the plot exists
to show.

[Transport](/Electrochemical-Cell-Simulator/physics/transport/) moves ions up to the
electrode surface; kinetics decides how fast they react once they arrive. In Voltaire the
reaction rate law is not a separate model — it is the boundary condition that couples the
potential and concentration fields in the electrolyte to the potential in the solid. Get it
wrong and every polarisation curve, every impedance arc, and every capacity prediction
downstream is wrong with it.

## The Butler–Volmer relation

The current density crossing an electrode–electrolyte interface is the difference between
an anodic (oxidising) and a cathodic (reducing) partial reaction, each accelerated
exponentially by the part of the interfacial potential step that tilts its activation
barrier:

<Equation id="eq-bv" latex="j = j_0\left[\exp\!\left(\frac{\alpha_a F \eta}{R T}\right) - \exp\!\left(-\frac{\alpha_c F \eta}{R T}\right)\right]" caption="Butler–Volmer relation: net interfacial current as the difference of anodic and cathodic partial currents." />

The driving force is the surface overpotential, the deviation of the actual potential step
across the interface from its equilibrium value:

<Equation id="eq-eta" latex="\eta = \phi_s - \phi_l - E^0" caption="Surface overpotential: how far the interfacial potential step sits from the equilibrium potential of the reaction." />

At $\eta = 0$ the two exponentials are both one and the net current vanishes — the
interface is at equilibrium, not at rest. Positive $\eta$ favours oxidation and drives
anodic current (positive $j$ by our sign convention); negative $\eta$ drives reduction.

## Rate parameters

### Exchange current density

At equilibrium the net current is zero, but the forward and reverse reactions both run —
each at rate $j_0$. The exchange current density is that hidden traffic, and it sets the
scale for everything the interface does: a reaction with large $j_0$ delivers useful
current at millivolts of overpotential, while one with small $j_0$ needs hundreds of
millivolts for the same current. Typical values span four orders of magnitude, roughly
1e-6 to 1e-2 A/cm²: oxygen reduction and evolution sit at the sluggish end of that range
on most surfaces, while hydrogen oxidation on platinum and many lithium intercalation
reactions sit near the facile end.

$j_0$ is not a constant of the reaction alone. It depends on the local concentrations of
reactants and products at the surface and rises with temperature in Arrhenius fashion, so
in a full cell model it varies along the electrode and over time as the electrolyte
depletes. The demo treats it as a slider; the solver treats it as a field.

### Transfer coefficients

The transfer coefficients $\alpha_a$ and $\alpha_c$ describe how the interfacial potential
step is shared between raising the anodic barrier and lowering the cathodic one. For a
single-electron transfer they normally sum to one, and the symmetric case
$\alpha_a = \alpha_c = 0.5$ — a barrier tilted equally both ways — is the default
assumption when measurements say nothing better. Asymmetric coefficients skew the
current–overpotential curve: the branch with the larger coefficient rises faster.

## Limiting regimes

The full expression is rarely what you see in a measurement. Two limits cover most
operating conditions, and knowing which one applies tells you what an experiment can and
cannot determine.

### Tafel behaviour

Once $|\eta|$ is large enough, one exponential dwarfs the other. Keeping only the anodic
branch and taking logarithms gives a straight line in semi-log coordinates:

<Equation id="eq-tafel" latex="\log_{10}|j| = \log_{10} j_0 + \frac{|\eta|}{b}, \qquad b = \frac{2.303\,R T}{\alpha F}" caption="Tafel line: the slope b is the overpotential cost of one decade of current." />

The slope $b$ is the number of volts of extra overpotential needed to multiply the current
by ten. At $\alpha = 0.5$ and 25 °C it is about 118 mV per decade; at $\alpha = 1$, about
59 mV per decade. Reading $b$ off a measured polarisation curve is the standard way to
extract $\alpha$, and extrapolating the line back to $\eta = 0$ yields $j_0$. The
approximation is good once the neglected reverse branch is negligible: its relative size
is $\exp(-(\alpha_a+\alpha_c)F\eta/RT)$, which for $\alpha_a + \alpha_c = 1$ at 25 °C
drops below 1% when $|\eta|$ exceeds roughly 118 mV.

### Micro-polarisation limit

Near equilibrium both exponentials can be expanded to first order and the interface
becomes a linear resistor:

<Equation id="eq-linear" latex="j \approx j_0\,\frac{(\alpha_a + \alpha_c)\,F}{R T}\,\eta" caption="Micro-polarisation limit: near equilibrium the interface responds linearly, like a resistor." />

The reciprocal slope is the area-specific charge-transfer resistance
$R_{ct} = RT/\big((\alpha_a+\alpha_c)F j_0\big)$ — about 25.7 Ω·cm² for
$j_0 = 10^{-3}$ A/cm² with $\alpha_a+\alpha_c = 1$ at 25 °C. This is the quantity an
impedance measurement reports as the diameter of the charge-transfer arc, which is how EIS
measures $j_0$ without ever driving the cell hard. The textbook rule of thumb keeps
$|\eta|$ below about 10 mV; using the sharper criterion shipped in the demo — the
overpotential at which the linearised current deviates from the full expression by 5% —
the linear window extends to about ±29 mV for symmetric coefficients at 25 °C. Between
the two regimes, from a few tens of millivolts up to roughly 100 mV, neither
approximation is trustworthy and the full expression must be used.

## Numerical treatment

<StatusBadge status="shipped" label="browser demo" /> <StatusBadge status="in-progress" label="Python solver" />

In the discretised cell model, Butler–Volmer enters as a nonlinear Robin-type boundary
condition: the normal ionic flux at the interface is fixed by $j$ through Faraday's law,
and $j$ in turn depends on the boundary values of the unknowns themselves — $\phi_s$,
$\phi_l$, and, through $j_0$, the local concentration. Voltaire does not lag or
hand-linearise this condition. The BV flux is written directly into the residual
$R(u)$, and the same automatic differentiation that assembles the transport Jacobian
$J = \partial R / \partial u$ differentiates it, so Newton sees the exponentials exactly:

<Equation id="eq-djdeta" latex="\frac{\partial j}{\partial \eta} = \frac{j_0 F}{R T}\left[\alpha_a \exp\!\left(\frac{\alpha_a F \eta}{R T}\right) + \alpha_c \exp\!\left(-\frac{\alpha_c F \eta}{R T}\right)\right]" caption="Exact sensitivity of the BV current to overpotential — the Jacobian entry the Newton solver consumes." />

This derivative grows as fast as the current itself, which is exactly why the solver uses
damped Newton: a full step taken far from equilibrium can overshoot into exponential
overflow, so the step is cut until the residual norm decreases. The same damped-Newton
pattern runs live in the browser in the
[double-layer demo](/Electrochemical-Cell-Simulator/demos/#demo-double-layer). Building
the boundary condition into the one shared Jacobian also pays off twice more: the
frequency-domain EIS solve $(i\omega M - J)\hat{u} = \hat{b}$ inherits the linearised
interface — the $1/R_{ct}$ conductance above — with no separate small-signal model, and
the adjoint solve $J^{\mathsf{T}}\lambda = -\partial Q/\partial u$ gets design
sensitivities through the kinetics for free.

## Validation

<StatusBadge status="shipped" label="browser demo" />

The [Butler–Volmer demo](/Electrochemical-Cell-Simulator/demos/#demo-butler-volmer)
evaluates the exact expression above in TypeScript, live, with sliders for $j_0$,
$\alpha_a$, $\alpha_c$, and $T$. In its Tafel view it overlays dashed asymptotes whose
labels report $b$ in mV per decade, computed from $b = 2.303RT/(\alpha F)$ at the
current slider values; the annotation therefore matches the formula by construction, and
the check is that the simulated curve converges onto those asymptotes at large
overpotential and peels away below roughly 100 mV where the reverse branch still
contributes. The linear view overlays the micro-polarisation line and marks the ±5%
deviation boundary of the linear window, computed by bisection against the full
expression. These are consistency checks of the implementation against exact analytic
limits, not comparisons with experimental data — see the
[validation page](/Electrochemical-Cell-Simulator/validation/) for the full list of what
has and has not been checked.

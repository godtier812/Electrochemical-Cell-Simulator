An isothermal cell model is a convenient fiction. Every rate coefficient in the
[transport](/Electrochemical-Cell-Simulator/physics/transport/) and
[kinetics](/Electrochemical-Cell-Simulator/physics/kinetics/) equations depends on
temperature, and every ampere the cell passes deposits heat that moves that temperature.
Voltaire treats the energy balance as one more conservation law in the same system —
solved simultaneously with concentration and potential, not applied as a correction
afterwards.

## The energy equation

The temperature field obeys a heat equation with conduction and three volumetric source
terms, one for each distinct way an operating cell generates (or absorbs) heat:

<Equation
  id="eq-energy"
  latex="\rho c_p \frac{\partial T}{\partial t} = \nabla \cdot \left( k \nabla T \right) + q_{\mathrm{ohm}} + q_{\mathrm{rxn}} + q_{\mathrm{rev}}"
  caption="Energy balance: thermal inertia, conduction, and the ohmic, irreversible, and reversible heat sources."
/>

The left side is the rate of change of stored thermal energy; the conduction term
redistributes heat down temperature gradients and carries it to the cooled boundary. What
remains is the source budget, split below.

  ]}
/>

## Heat sources

### Ohmic heat

<Equation
  id="eq-q-ohm"
  latex="q_{\mathrm{ohm}} = I^{2} R"
  caption="Joule heating: current forced through ohmic resistance."
/>

This is Joule's law: the dissipation from driving current through the resistive parts of
the cell — electrolyte, solid conductors, and contacts — written in its familiar lumped
form; in the distributed model the same dissipation is evaluated per unit volume from the
local current and the local effective resistance. It is always positive and scales with
the square of the current, which is why it dominates the heat budget at high rates.

### Irreversible reaction heat

<Equation
  id="eq-q-rxn"
  latex="q_{\mathrm{rxn}} = j \, \eta"
  caption="Irreversible reaction heat: interfacial current times the overpotential that drives it."
/>

Pushing charge across the interface at a finite rate costs an overpotential $\eta$, and
the product $j\eta$ is the power that cost dissipates. Because
[Butler–Volmer kinetics](/Electrochemical-Cell-Simulator/physics/kinetics/) gives $j$
and $\eta$ the same sign — you can see the odd symmetry directly in the
[Butler–Volmer demo](/Electrochemical-Cell-Simulator/demos/#demo-butler-volmer) — this
term is dissipative in both charge and discharge.

### Reversible entropic heat

<Equation
  id="eq-q-rev"
  latex="q_{\mathrm{rev}} = j \, T \, \frac{\partial E^{0}}{\partial T}"
  caption="Reversible heat: the entropy change of the cell reaction, proportional to current."
/>

The cell reaction changes the entropy of the electrodes, and that entropy change is
exchanged with the surroundings as heat at rate $jT(\partial E^{0}/\partial T)$. Unlike
the other two sources this one is not a loss, and it changes sign between charge and
discharge: $j$ reverses while the entropic coefficient does not, so a reaction that heats
the cell on discharge cools it on charge. It is also the only source that is linear in
current — the ohmic and irreversible terms both scale roughly as $j^2$ near equilibrium —
so at low rates the entropic term decays slowest and can set the sign of the net heat
flow.

## Temperature feedback

Heat generation would be a one-way street if the coefficients were constants. They are
not. The diffusion coefficients, the ionic conductivity, and the exchange current density
all follow Arrhenius laws:

<Equation
  id="eq-arrhenius"
  latex="X(T) = X_{\mathrm{ref}} \, \exp\!\left[ \frac{E_{a,X}}{R} \left( \frac{1}{T_{\mathrm{ref}}} - \frac{1}{T} \right) \right], \qquad X \in \{ D_i,\ \kappa,\ j_0 \}"
  caption="Arrhenius temperature correction applied to each transport and kinetic coefficient."
/>

Each coefficient carries its own activation energy, so a temperature rise speeds up
diffusion, conduction, and interfacial kinetics by different factors. In this equation
$R$ is the molar gas constant, not the resistance of the ohmic term above.

<SymbolTable
  caption="Symbols in the Arrhenius law"
  rows={[
    { s: 'X_{\\mathrm{ref}}', meaning: 'coefficient value at the reference temperature', units: 'units of X' },
    { s: 'E_{a,X}', meaning: 'activation energy of coefficient X', units: 'J/mol', typical: '2e4 – 6e4' },
    { s: 'T_{\\mathrm{ref}}', meaning: 'reference temperature', units: 'K', typical: '298.15' },
    { s: 'R', meaning: 'molar gas constant', units: 'J/(mol·K)', typical: '8.314' },
  ]}
/>

This closes a loop. Current makes heat; heat raises $T$; a higher $T$ raises $D_i$,
$\kappa$, and $j_0$; faster transport and kinetics let more current flow at the same
voltage; more current makes more heat. Whether that loop settles or diverges is decided
by the competition between the source terms and heat removal — conduction through the
stack and cooling at the cell surface. When removal wins, the cell finds a steady
operating temperature. When generation wins, the loop runs away: this is the essential
mechanism of thermal runaway, and a model can only represent it if the temperature
dependence feeds back into the same equations that generate the heat. A model that
computes temperature from a frozen electrochemical solution cannot run away, whatever
the cell does.

We do not show simulated runaway curves on this page. The thermal block has not yet
produced validated results, and a dramatic plot without a validation trail would be
decoration. See [validation](/Electrochemical-Cell-Simulator/validation/) for what has
and has not been checked.

## One residual, one Jacobian

The coupling strategy is deliberately boring: temperature is appended to the unknown
vector, and the energy balance is appended to the residual. With
$u = (C_1, \ldots, C_N, \phi, T)$ the solver assembles a single residual $R(u)$ and
differentiates it once:

<Equation
  id="eq-jacobian-blocks"
  latex="J = \frac{\partial R}{\partial u} = \begin{pmatrix} \partial_C R_C & \partial_\phi R_C & \partial_T R_C \\ \partial_C R_\phi & \partial_\phi R_\phi & \partial_T R_\phi \\ \partial_C R_T & \partial_\phi R_T & \partial_T R_T \end{pmatrix}"
  caption="Block structure of the Jacobian once temperature joins the unknown vector."
/>

The last column holds the Arrhenius feedback — how the transport and kinetics residuals
move when $T$ moves — and the last row holds the heat sources, which depend on
concentration through $j$ and on potential through $\eta$ and the ohmic dissipation.
None of these cross-terms is derived by hand: the Jacobian comes from automatic
differentiation of the assembled residual, so adding the thermal block means writing its
residual and nothing else. Damped Newton then solves the fully coupled system, which
matters here because the runaway loop is exactly the kind of stiff feedback that a
segregated scheme — solve electrochemistry, then update temperature, then repeat — lags
by a timestep and can misjudge.

The same augmented Jacobian is what the rest of the architecture consumes: the
frequency-domain impedance solve $(i\omega M - J)\hat{u} = \hat{b}$ and the adjoint
sensitivity solve $J^{\mathsf{T}}\lambda = -\partial Q / \partial u$ apply to the
enlarged blocks unchanged, so thermal effects will enter impedance spectra and design
sensitivities through the same machinery once the block lands.

## Status

The energy equation and its Arrhenius coupling are being implemented in the Python
solver, which is planned and not yet written. None of the browser demos
solves the coupled energy equation. Thermal validation — analytic conduction benchmarks
and a comparison against PyBaMM's thermal P2D model — has not yet been run. Progress is
tracked on the [roadmap](/Electrochemical-Cell-Simulator/roadmap/).
